01

The problem

Platform tennis is played outdoors, in winter, inside a wire cage — a brutal environment for sports video. Clubs wanted match replays, close line calls, coaching film, and stats, but nothing existed short of a human with a camera. The court is reflective, the ball is small and fast, players occlude each other constantly, and whatever runs has to survive snow, glare, and zero on-site staff.

02

The pipeline

Two industrial Axis cameras watch each court from opposite ends. From there, every stage is purpose-built:

Capture QR-activated · Axis cameras

Members activate recording by scanning a QR code at the court — no staff, no app install, no camera operator. An experimental 180 KB int8 detector also runs on the camera's own AI chip via Axis ACAP.

Sync & calibration Dual-view geometry

The views are time-aligned from the physics of the game itself — sub-frame bounce changepoints matched across cameras, with rolling-shutter skew explicitly modeled. Both cameras are calibrated jointly against the court's known geometry, and three physics assertions hard-fail the run if the world comes out wrong.

Perception TensorRT · 1.4× realtime

Ball tracking every frame, pose at 15 Hz, court keypoints at 1 Hz — over both views concurrently at 84 fps per view on a single RTX 4090 (51 fps per view with the paddle worker running). The overlay video above is this stage drawn onto real match footage.

3D flight solving The trajectory is the event detector

Dual-view detections triangulate into a 3D ball track, and ballistic arcs are fitted to it. Every deviation is an impulse that classifies itself by geometry: a sign flip at z ≈ 0 is a bounce, an impulse at a cage plane is a screen, mid-air is a paddle contact. Moving events into 3D needed zero training labels.

Identity & transcript Court coordinates, not pixels

A ReID-first tracker holds four fixed player identities across both views in court feet — including through changeovers — and flights, contacts, and poses become a shot-by-shot transcript: serve, drive, lob, overhead, volley, attributed to players and linked to video.

Product paddlescreens.com

A web app where members watch dual-view replays, jump through the shot log, filter by player or shot type, review stats, and challenge line calls. Clubs get live streams and a full video-coaching workspace.

03

Deployed at real clubs

The models are half the job. The other half is keeping twenty cameras across eight clubs working through a platform-tennis winter — outdoors, with no IT staff on site and consumer routers, where every upgrade is a drive.

The recorder lives on the camera

A C program running on each Axis camera as an ACAP app. It only ever calls out over HTTPS — polling for jobs, exporting from the SD card, streaming straight to the video host — so nothing at the club needs an open port behind its router. It reports its version for fleet visibility, and because cameras can’t update themselves, releases are planned around site visits.

Cutovers are reversible

Moving a club onto the on-camera path is one script, and it refuses to run unless every camera at that club has sent a heartbeat in the last three minutes. A single flag rolls the club back.

Monitoring written from incidents

A club’s recordings once failed silently for two days, until a member noticed; nine recorders were offline for a month before anyone checked. Now a scheduled check pushes an alert within half an hour, once per incident, and live streams are watched minute by minute.

Debugging real networks

Club routers dropped RTMP streams roughly every fifty seconds, which pushed live video testing to SRT. Cameras record 16 kHz audio that YouTube silently discards, so the streaming relay resamples it to 44.1 kHz before it goes out.

04

Line calls, solved in 3D

When a call is challenged, the solver fits gravity-constrained flight paths through the frames before and after the bounce, solves where the ball's contact patch touched the deck, and renders the verdict — with its own uncertainty on screen. Calls are reported as a probability, not a pronouncement: per-axis confidence is sub-inch, and the independent before/after solutions are required to agree.

A solved challenge: 57 ft/s at 23° descent, contact mark 3.22 × 1.54 in — IN by 1.39 in, with the before/after solutions agreeing to 2.3 in.

The flyover render members see on a challenge — the camera orbits the solved trajectory into the bounce mark.

05

The models

Roughly twenty trained models across eight families, every one evaluated — and nearly all trained — on footage from the real installed cameras, not public datasets. The deployment ladder runs from batch processing on a home RTX 4090, to realtime streaming on AWS T4s, to a Jetson Orin Nano at the court, down to a 180 KB int8 model inside the camera itself.

Ball tracking

Five-frame detection tuned for a small, fast ball against wire fencing and snow. F1 .928 at precision .970 on a human-labeled 65-view benchmark that includes deliberately adversarial views; TensorRT export verified at 100% parity within 3 px.

Court keypoints

Finds all 18 court keypoints with a mean error of 1.19 px — the geometric anchor that calibration, 3D solving, and line calls all build on.

Player pose

Full-body keypoints for all four players at 15 Hz, tracked through occlusion as they cross, screen each other, and play off the cage.

Paddle detection

A genuinely small-object problem — the median paddle is 42 px square. The production detector reaches box mAP50 .910 and cut worst-venue contact-frame error from 106 px to 23 px.

Shot classification

Labels every stroke — drive, volley, lob, overhead — at .954 accuracy on held-out folds, powering the searchable match transcript.

Identity tracking

Four fixed player identities held across both views in court coordinates — a seven-minute match segment tracked with zero identity switches and both changeovers detected, at 0.31 ms per sample.

Phantom filter

Score and geometry gates that cut false ball detections from 34.9 to 3.4 per thousand kept — because one phantom can poison an entire 3D solve.

Edge models

Distilled edge exports — down to a 40 KB ball net — for the Jetson at the court and the camera's own DLPU, where a 180 KB int8 model already runs.

  • The most defensible headline: .785 F1 for whole-video event detection on a held-out match, scored with no silencing whatsoever. The most interesting document in the repo is the self-audit that lowered its own previously published headline after finding the metric had been quietly forgiven 39% of the footage.
  • Geometry beat the model. Moving event detection from single-view 2D to dual-view 3D bought +24 points of contact recall and +57 points of bounce recall — with zero additional training labels.
  • Every metric gets tested on a known-wrong change. That rule exists because a pixel-perfect 1.19 px court fit once placed the camera 12.7 ft below the deck — while the reprojection metrics all still read healthy.
  • Scale: 34,644 hand-labeled ball frames across 139 matches, ~2,700 hand-corrected event labels across 13 clips, 7+ venues in summer and winter light, and 85+ written experiment reports behind the shipped choices.
  • The open gaps are written down too: automatic rally segmentation is the real remaining gap, and a degraded input stage can look like a perfectly normal match — which is why every run now emits a certificate that refuses to ship events when a stage is broken.
07

How it was built

The entire program — camera firmware to cloud pipeline to product — is built by one engineer running fleets of AI coding agents: research, implementation, and adversarial review in parallel, with hard verification gates before anything merges. Training jobs stream checkpoints to durable storage so every epoch is testable mid-run; model claims are attacked by independent review agents before they're believed. It's a working demonstration of what one person ships when the workflow is engineered as carefully as the product.
